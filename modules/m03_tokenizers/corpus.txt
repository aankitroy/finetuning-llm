The baker woke before the sun, as she did every morning. The street outside was quiet and the ovens were cold. She lit the first oven, tied on her apron, and took the starter down from the shelf. The starter was older than the shop. Her grandmother had fed it every day for forty years, and now she fed it every day too.

Bread is simple. Flour, water, salt and time. The flour gives the bread its body, the water wakes the flour up, the salt gives it taste, and time does the rest. A baker who rushes the time gets a heavy loaf. A baker who waits gets a light one, with a crisp crust and an open crumb.

She mixed the dough by hand. She folded it, turned it, and folded it again. After an hour the dough was smooth and soft. After two hours it had doubled. She shaped the loaves on the wooden table, one by one, and set them in their baskets to rise once more.

At six the first customers came. The teacher came for a small brown loaf. The doctor came for two white rolls. The old man from the corner came for whatever was warmest, and he always said the same thing: "This is the best bread in the town." She always smiled and said the same thing back: "It is the same bread as yesterday."

But it was never quite the same. The weather changed the dough. On a warm day the dough rose quickly, and she had to watch it closely. On a cold day it rose slowly, and she had to wait. On a wet day the flour drank less water, and on a dry day it drank more. Every day she had to taste, touch and watch, and every day the bread was a little different.

Her daughter asked her once how she knew when the bread was ready. "You listen," she said. "Tap the bottom of the loaf. If it sounds hollow, it is ready. If it sounds heavy, give it five more minutes." Her daughter tapped a loaf and listened. It sounded hollow. "Ready," she said, and the baker laughed. "Ready," she agreed.

In the afternoon the shop was quiet again. The baker cleaned the tables, swept the floor, and fed the starter. She wrote down what she had baked and what she had sold. Forty loaves of brown bread, thirty loaves of white bread, sixty rolls, and twelve sweet buns for the school. She wrote down the weather too, because the weather mattered.

In the evening she made the dough for the next morning and left it to rise slowly in the cool room at the back of the shop. Then she locked the door, walked home along the river, and went to bed early. Tomorrow the ovens would be cold again, and she would light them again, and the bread would be the same, and a little different.

The town grew. New houses were built along the river, and new people came to the shop. Some of them wanted bread with seeds, so she made bread with seeds. Some of them wanted bread without salt, so she made a little of that too. A family from the south asked for flat bread, and showed her how their grandmother had made it. She made it the next day, and the day after that, until it was right.

Years later her daughter ran the shop. She woke before the sun, lit the first oven, and took the starter down from the shelf. The starter was older than the shop, older than her mother, and older than the town's new houses. She fed it, as her mother had fed it, and her grandmother before that. Bread is simple, she thought. Flour, water, salt and time.
